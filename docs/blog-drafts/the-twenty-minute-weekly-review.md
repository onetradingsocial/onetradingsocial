Most traders review their trading after a bad day and not at all after a good one. That is backwards, and not because of discipline. It is backwards because the days you feel most like reviewing are the days you are least able to read honestly, and because a single day is too small to hold a pattern.

This is not an argument against the daily habit. [Why a trading journal is the highest-ROI habit you're not building](/blog/why-a-trading-journal) makes the case for a ten-minute daily loop, and that case stands: the daily pass is for **capture** — logging the trade, the thesis and the state you were in while you still remember them. The weekly pass is for **conclusions**. Capture daily, judge weekly; the two jobs want different sample sizes and different distances from the outcome.

What follows is the second job, in about twenty minutes. It is an order rather than a checklist, because with this material the order does most of the work.

## Why conclusions wait for the week

Three reasons, in ascending order of how much they matter.

The first is arithmetic. If you take three trades a day, a day's worth of evidence is a three-trade sample. Almost nothing you can observe in three trades separates a tendency from chance. Five sessions of that is fifteen trades, which is still small, but it is the first point at which "I keep doing this" is a statement about repetition rather than about one afternoon.

The second is that you are not a neutral reader of your own day. Baron and Hershey's 1988 experiment gave people identical decisions with different outcomes attached and asked them to rate the decision quality; the decisions with good outcomes were rated better. It has since been replicated with 692 participants and a larger effect than the original. A daily review is conducted in the presence of one very loud outcome, and the outcome will colour your judgement of the process whether or not you believe it should.

The third is that how often you look at results appears to change what you do next, separately from what the results say. Thaler, Tversky, Kahneman and Schwartz (1997) found that participants given less frequent feedback on an investment allocation task took more risk than those given frequent feedback. Be careful with this one: it is a laboratory task about portfolio allocation, not trade review, and "took more risk" is a finding, not a recommendation. The transferable part is narrower and still useful — evaluation frequency is itself an input to behaviour, so it is worth choosing deliberately rather than by mood.

## Minutes 0 to 4: count things that are not money

Open the week and count. How many trades. How many days you traded. How many of those trades were setups you had named in advance, and how many were not.

That is all. No judgement yet, and no P&L. The point of starting here is that these are the numbers you can compare to your own previous weeks without needing to know whether the week was good, and they are usually where the honest answer already is. A week with nineteen trades when you normally take eleven has told you something before you have looked at a single result.

## Minutes 4 to 9: rule compliance before results

Next: what share of the week's trades followed the rules you had already set — your maximum risk per trade, your cap on trades per day, your minimum planned reward-to-risk, whether a stop was on from the start.

Measure adherence and stop there. Do not yet ask whether the rules are any good; that is a separate question with a much longer time horizon, and answering it in the same five minutes is how people quietly loosen rules that were working. The useful output here is a fraction and a list, not a verdict.

## Minutes 9 to 14: two trades, reopened

Pick exactly two: the largest loser, and the trade you would least want to explain to somebody else. They are often not the same trade, and the second one is usually the more informative.

For each, ask one question: was this a bad decision, or a bad outcome? A trade that followed your plan and lost is not a lesson. A trade that broke your plan and won is. This is the question outcome bias makes hardest to answer honestly, which is why it goes in writing rather than in your head, and why it is asked on a Sunday rather than four minutes after the position closed.

Do not review your best trade. It is the least instructive thing in the week and it eats the clock.

## Minutes 14 to 17: one number, in R

Look at the running expectancy of your record, expressed in R, not the week's P&L in currency. The [expectancy calculator](/tools/expectancy-calculator) does the arithmetic, and [win rate is lying to you](/blog/win-rate-is-lying-to-you) covers why the headline percentage is not the thing that decides whether you make money.

The caveat matters more than the number: a single week is far too small a sample for expectancy to mean anything on its own. You are checking whether the running figure across your whole record has moved, and mostly it will not have. A metric that does not move most weeks is doing its job.

## Minutes 17 to 20: write one change

End by writing one sentence. Not a resolution — a rule you will either have kept or not kept by next Sunday.

"Be more patient" is not a rule; nothing can be scored against it. "No entries in the first five minutes of the session" is a rule. So is "no second trade on an instrument that has already stopped me out today". Those two are arbitrary examples, not suggestions — the content of the rule is yours, and the only test here is whether a row in next week's journal can be marked as following it or not.

## What to ignore

- **The week's P&L as a verdict.** It is data. It is not a grade.
- **Any pattern with too few trades behind it.** If you cannot say how many trades a claim rests on, you do not yet have the claim. TradingSocial's personalised insights (Pro) state the sample size for exactly this reason — four trades and a hunch look identical to a real tendency without it.
- **Other people's weeks.** Their position sizes, instruments and constraints are not yours, so the comparison carries no information.
- **Anything you cannot turn into a rule.** If an observation cannot be written as something you either did or did not do, it will not survive contact with Tuesday.

## Why one change and not ten

A review that produces ten changes produces none, for a reason that has nothing to do with willpower. Change five things at once and next month's results cannot tell you which of the five did anything. You have spent a month and bought no information.

One change per week is fifty-two changes a year, each of them attributable. That is a faster rate of actual learning than any amount of Sunday-night resolve. "Pick one lesson, not ten" is the daily version of the same instruction in [why a trading journal](/blog/why-a-trading-journal); what the weekly cadence adds is that a week is long enough for the last change to have been tested before the next one lands. It is the same logic behind [tagging your mistakes](/blog/how-to-tag-your-trading-mistakes) rather than simply resolving not to repeat them.

## The honest limit

A weekly review will not make you profitable. It makes your record legible, which is a smaller claim and the only one worth making. Whether there is an edge in there to find is a separate question, and the review is how you would find out — not a substitute for the answer.

Twenty minutes, same time each week, in the same order, whether the week was green or red. The weeks you least want to open are the ones with the most in them.
